\section{Introduction}

This document presents the formal task model used in our research on multi-mode
mixed-criticality (MC) scheduling. The model extends traditional two-mode
Adaptive Mixed-Criticality (AMC) scheduling to support multiple criticality
levels, enabling graded degradation rather than a single normal/degraded
transition.

\textbf{Key analysis principle.} Every degradation level has its own execution
budget, not just normal and fully-degraded. What makes the scheme analysable is
not that only two budgets exist -- they do not -- but that each level's
worst-case execution time (WCET) budget is converted, via the \emph{same}
response-time analysis used for classic two-level AMC, into a worst-case
response time (WCRT) that is monitored at run time. Reaching that WCRT is what
triggers the transition into that level. Safety is preserved because each
level's operating point -- its budget together with the set of LO-criticality
tasks it keeps running -- is checked at design time against exactly this
analysis, generalised across levels rather than restricted to two
(\Cref{sec:admissibility}).

\textbf{What this revision adds.} Three changes, each motivated by a worked
example in \Cref{sec:motivating} and each with a consequence that is not
cosmetic:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{shed-aware} response time $R_i(\chi, S, s)$
  (\Cref{sec:shed-aware}) that charges a shed task for the jobs it released
  before it stopped. It specialises to the normal-mode bound, to the severity
  trigger, and to AMC-rtb exactly, so it subsumes rather than replaces them --
  and it makes the instant of shedding an argument of the analysis, which is
  what lets the analysis prefer shedding early.
\item An \emph{allocated criticality level} $\ell_i$
  (\Cref{sec:criticality-levels}) ordering tasks within the LO-criticality tier
  as well as across the HI/LO divide, because response-time analysis certifies
  which sets are safe to shed but cannot rank the safe ones.
\item A \emph{generalised trigger} (\Cref{sec:transition}) under which any task
  a rung would protect may demand that rung, with a parameter
  $x_{\mathrm{LO}}$ bounding how deep a LO-criticality task may reach. This
  detects overruns that the two-level trigger provably cannot see, at the cost
  of a containment guarantee that must be restated rather than assumed.
\item \emph{Deadline termination} and the JNC objective
  (\Cref{sec:termination,sec:objective}). A LO-criticality job that cannot
  finish in time is discarded at its deadline rather than allowed to complete
  late, since a stale result may be worse than none. This removes the
  LO-criticality deadline obligation entirely -- there is nothing left to miss
  -- which turns clause 2 of the admissibility criterion from a constraint into
  an objective and raises LO-criticality retention from $31.7\%$ to $52.4\%$
  with no change to HI-criticality safety.
\end{enumerate}

Two findings are consequential and negative, and are reported as such.
\emph{Progressive} shedding cannot be certified on $47\%$ of an otherwise
schedulable population once shedding is charged honestly, while shedding the
same tasks at the shallowest rung is always certifiable and never sheds more
(\Cref{sec:admissibility}); grading therefore belongs in the budgets, not the
drop sets. And the generalised trigger, though it targets a blind spot that
swallows $87$--$89\%$ of all overruns, changes the service ratio by under
$0.05$ percentage points, because a single drop set leaves escalation with
nothing further to shed (\Cref{sec:transition}).

The model builds upon the foundational work presented in~\cite{9804600}.

\section{Motivating Examples}
\label{sec:motivating}

Three situations that classic two-level AMC handles badly, each motivating one
feature of the model that follows. They are worked out concretely rather than
described, because in every case the argument turns on the gap between a
worst-case response-time \emph{bound} and the response time actually observed.
Every schedule and every number below is reproduced by the accompanying
implementation.

\subsection{Example 1: deferring the shed starves the task it protects}
\label{sec:ex-defer}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Task & $\kappa_i$ & Priority & $T_i = D_i$ & $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ & $C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ \\
\midrule
$\tau_1$ & LO & 0 (high) & 10  & 4 & -- \\
$\tau_2$ & HI & 1        & 50  & 8 & 20 \\
$\tau_3$ & LO & 2 (low)  & 100 & 5 & -- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Here $R_2(0) = R_2^{\mathrm{lo}} = 16$ and $R_2(1) = 36$, so the
shallowest rung fires at $t = 16$ and the deepest at $t = 36$. Suppose
$\tau_2$ exhibits HI-criticality behaviour and executes its full
$C_2^{\mathrm{hi}} = 20$, with every task released at $t = 0$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
(a) nothing shed & \texttt{1111222222111122222211112222221111223333} \\
(b) $\tau_1$ shed at $t = 16$ & \texttt{111122222211112222222222222233333\ldots\ldots.} \\
 & \texttt{0~~~~~~~~~10~~~~~~~~20~~~~~~~~30~~~~~~~~40} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent In (a) $\tau_2$ completes at $t = 36$: it is preempted for four ticks
in every ten by $\tau_1$, so it accrues execution at $6/10$ of real time and
takes $36$ ticks of wall-clock to consume $20$ ticks of budget. It therefore
\emph{reaches the deepest rung at the very instant it finishes}, and the
deepest rung abandons every LO-criticality task -- $\tau_3$ included, which was
never the problem.

In (b) the single higher-priority LO-criticality task is shed at the
\emph{shallowest} rung, at $t = 16$. $\tau_2$ then runs uninterrupted and
completes at $t = 28$, so $R_2(1) = 36$ is never reached and the deepest rung
never fires. The cost is four abandoned releases of $\tau_1$; the saving is
that $\tau_3$ is not abandoned at all and the system never fully degrades.

The point is not that $\tau_2$ needed a larger budget -- it was entitled to
$C_2^{\mathrm{hi}}$ throughout, and (b) does not give it one tick more. It is
that a run-time trigger measures \emph{elapsed time} while a job's progress is
\emph{execution time}, and higher-priority LO-criticality work drives a wedge
between the two. Shedding that work early converts elapsed time into progress.
This is why \Cref{sec:shed-aware} makes the shed instant an argument of the
response-time bound, and why \Cref{sec:admissibility} concludes that shedding
belongs at the shallowest rung.

\subsection{Example 2: safety does not determine which tasks to keep}
\label{sec:ex-order}

Task set seed 7 of the generator ($n = 6$, $U = 0.7$, $CF = 2$), priorities by
Audsley's algorithm:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Task & $\kappa_i$ & Priority & $T_i = D_i$ & $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ & $C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ \\
\midrule
$\tau_5$ & LO & 0 & 398  & 64   & --   \\
$\tau_4$ & LO & 1 & 282  & 38   & --   \\
$\tau_3$ & HI & 2 & 3559 & 164  & 423  \\
$\tau_1$ & HI & 3 & 1779 & 2    & 5    \\
$\tau_2$ & HI & 4 & 6229 & 496  & 3605 \\
$\tau_6$ & LO & 5 & 5586 & 1547 & --   \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Enumerating every subset of $\{\tau_4, \tau_5, \tau_6\}$ against the
admissibility criterion of \Cref{sec:admissibility} yields exactly two
\emph{minimal} admissible drop sets:

\[
\{\tau_4\} \;\text{(keeping } \tau_5, \tau_6\text{)}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\{\tau_5\} \;\text{(keeping } \tau_4, \tau_6\text{)} .
\]

Two-level AMC sheds all three. The graded scheme can keep two -- but
\emph{which} two is not a question the analysis answers: both choices are
equally safe, and both retain the same number of tasks. $\tau_4$ and $\tau_5$
are interchangeable to response-time analysis and are not interchangeable to
the application. The shedding heuristics of \Cref{sec:admissibility} disagree
accordingly.

This is the gap an \emph{allocated criticality level} fills. Response-time
analysis certifies that a set is safe to shed; it cannot rank the safe sets.
\Cref{sec:criticality-levels} therefore replaces the binary
$\kappa_i \in \{\mathrm{HI}, \mathrm{LO}\}$ with a level $\ell_i$ that orders
tasks within the LO-criticality tier as well as across the divide.

\subsection{Example 3: an overrun the two-level trigger cannot see}
\label{sec:ex-blindspot}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Task & $\kappa_i$ & Priority & $T_i = D_i$ & $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ & $C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ \\
\midrule
$\tau_1$ & LO & 0 (high) & 10 & 3 & -- \\
$\tau_2$ & HI & 1        & 20 & 2 & 4  \\
$\tau_3$ & LO & 2 (low)  & 40 & 5 & -- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent $R_2^{\mathrm{lo}} = 5$ and $R_3^{\mathrm{lo}} = 10$. Let $\tau_2$
and $\tau_3$ be released at $t = 0$, let $\tau_1$'s next release fall at
$t = 5$ (its previous job ran in $[-5, -2)$, so the processor is idle at
$t = -1$ and $\tau_3$'s priority level-3 busy period genuinely begins at
$t = 0$), and let $\tau_2$ execute its full $C_2^{\mathrm{hi}} = 4$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l}
\toprule
\texttt{2222~3111~3333~...~111..} \\
\texttt{0123~4567~8901~234~56789} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent $\tau_2$ \emph{overruns} -- it executes $C_2^{\mathrm{hi}}$, twice its
normal budget -- yet completes at $t = 4$, before its own trigger
$R_2^{\mathrm{lo}} = 5$. Under two-level AMC, and under every rung of the
ladder as stated in \Cref{sec:transition}, \textbf{the system never degrades at
all}, because only a HI-criticality task may pull the trigger and this one beat
its own bound. It beat it because $R_2^{\mathrm{lo}}$ budgets for a full
interference term from $\tau_1$ that did not materialise in this release.

The overrun did not vanish; it landed on $\tau_3$, which at $t = 10 =
R_3^{\mathrm{lo}}$ has executed $3$ of its $5$ ticks and completes at $t = 12$.
A LO-criticality task has slack of its own, but it is the last to absorb the
error and the first to run out.

So $\tau_3$ can detect an overrun that $\tau_2$ concealed. Letting it act on
that detection -- shedding a lower-ranked LO-criticality task to protect
itself -- is the second mechanism this model adds (\Cref{sec:transition}). It
comes at a price that must be stated plainly: the scheme now degrades at
instants when two-level AMC does not, so the guarantee that it never abandons
more LO-criticality work than AMC-RH is \emph{false} in general. This example
is the counterexample, and \Cref{sec:transition}'s parameter $x_{\mathrm{LO}}$
is the dial that trades one against the other.

\section{Task Model}

\subsection{Basic Task Properties}

A task set $\tau = \{\tau_1, \tau_2, \dots, \tau_n\}$ consists of $n$
independent, sporadically arriving tasks. Each task $\tau_i$ is characterized
by:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Task Properties}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Symbol & Name & Description \\
\midrule
$T_i \in \N^+$ & Period & Minimum inter-arrival time (ticks) \\
$D_i \in \N^+$ & Deadline & Relative deadline ($D_i = T_i$ for implicit deadlines) \\
$C_i^{\mathrm{lo}} \in \N^+$ & Normal budget & Execution budget in normal mode \\
$C_i^{\mathrm{hi}} \in \N^+$ & HI execution budget & Execution budget once fully degraded ($C_i^{\mathrm{hi}} \geq C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$) \\
$\mathrm{BCET}_i \in \N^+$ & Best-case execution time & $\mathrm{BCET}_i \leq C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Severity and Per-Level Budgets}
\label{sec:severity-budgets}

Every degradation level has its own execution budget. Rather than name each
level's budget independently, we index it by a \emph{severity}
$\chi \in [0, 1]$ and interpolate:

\[
C_i(\chi) = \max\bigl(C_i^{\mathrm{lo}},\; C_i^{\mathrm{lo}} + \chi \cdot (C_i^{\mathrm{hi}} - C_i^{\mathrm{lo}})\bigr)
\]

so that $C_i(0) = C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$, $C_i(1) = C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$, and $\chi$
between the two endpoints gives an intermediate budget. The outer $\max$ is not
decorative: it is what guarantees $C_i(\chi) \geq C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ for every
$\chi$, which in turn is what makes the response times derived from it never
earlier than the normal-mode response time (\Cref{prop:c} below) -- rounding
must never be allowed to violate it. Linear interpolation is a design choice,
not a derivation: every property in this document holds for any interpolation
that is monotone in $\chi$ and never below $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$, so a
better-motivated member of that family is an open question, not a constraint
this analysis depends on.

\subsection{Criticality Assignment}
\label{sec:criticality-levels}

Each task carries the conventional binary criticality
\[
\kappa_i \in \{\mathrm{HI}, \mathrm{LO}\}
\]
with $\tau^{\mathrm{HI}} = \{\tau_i \in \tau \mid \kappa_i = \mathrm{HI}\}$ and
$\tau^{\mathrm{LO}} = \{\tau_i \in \tau \mid \kappa_i = \mathrm{LO}\}$. This
governs the \emph{budget}: only a HI-criticality task has
$C_i^{\mathrm{hi}} > C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$, and only a HI-criticality task has a
deadline obligation once fully degraded.

It does not, however, say anything about \emph{order of sacrifice} within the
LO-criticality tier, and \Cref{sec:ex-order} shows that response-time analysis
alone cannot supply one: a task set there admits two minimal drop sets of equal
size, both provably safe, differing only in which LO-criticality task survives.
So each task additionally carries an \emph{allocated criticality level}
\[
\ell_i \in \{1, \dots, k-1\},
\]
read as: \emph{$\tau_i$ runs at levels $L_1, \dots, L_{\ell_i}$ and is
abandoned at $L_{\ell_i + 1}$}. Every HI-criticality task has
$\ell_i = k-1$, since it is never abandoned. The allocation induces the drop
sets directly,
\[
S_x = \{\, i \mid \ell_i < x \,\},
\]
which are nested by construction, so choosing the allocation and choosing the
ladder of drop sets are the same act. Allocating those levels well is the
optimisation this work exists to study; the present document fixes only what
makes an allocation \emph{admissible} (\Cref{sec:admissibility}).

\subsection{Utilisation}

The utilisation of task $\tau_i$ under different criticality modes is:

\[
U_i^{\mathrm{lo}} = \frac{C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}}{T_i}, \quad
U_i^{\mathrm{hi}} = \begin{cases}
    \frac{C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}}{T_i} & \text{if } \kappa_i = \mathrm{HI} \\
    0 & \text{if } \kappa_i = \mathrm{LO}
\end{cases}
\]

The total LO-criticality utilisation is $U = \sum_{i=1}^n U_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$.
The HI-criticality utilisation factor (CF) relates aggregate utilisations:
\[
U^{\mathrm{hi}} = \sum_{\tau_i \in \tau^{\mathrm{HI}}} U_i^{\mathrm{hi}}, \quad
\text{where } U^{\mathrm{hi}} = CP \cdot CF \cdot U
\]
Here $CP = |\tau^{\mathrm{HI}}| / n$ is the criticality proportion.

\section{Response Time Analysis}
\label{sec:rta}

This section presents the standard fixed-priority response-time analysis, then
generalises it to a severity $\chi$. It is placed before the degradation model
(\Cref{sec:multilevel}) because that model's trigger points and safety argument
are both stated in terms of the response times defined here.

\subsection{Normal-Mode Response Time}
\label{sec:r-lo}

For fixed-priority preemptive scheduling, the response time of task $\tau_i$
under normal-mode budgets is the least fixed point of
\[
R_i^{\mathrm{lo}} = C_i^{\mathrm{lo}} + \sum_{\tau_j \in \mathrm{hp}(i)} \left\lceil \frac{R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}}{T_j} \right\rceil C_j^{\mathrm{lo}}
\]
where $\mathrm{hp}(i)$ is the set of tasks with higher priority than $\tau_i$.
\textbf{Requirement R1}, that a task set meets every deadline when every job
complies with its LO budget, holds exactly when $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}} \leq D_i$ for
every $\tau_i \in \tau$.

\subsection{Classic Two-Level Schedulability (AMC-rtb)}
\label{sec:amc-rtb}

The design-time question "can this task set tolerate full degradation at
all?" is answered independently of any run-time trigger, by the AMC-rtb test.
For a HI-criticality task $\tau_i$, the response time once every LO-criticality
task has been abandoned is the least fixed point of
\[
R_i^{\mathrm{hi}} = C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}
  + \sum_{\tau_j \in \mathrm{hp}_{\mathrm{HI}}(i)} \left\lceil \frac{R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}}{T_j} \right\rceil C_j^{\mathrm{hi}}
  + \sum_{\tau_k \in \mathrm{hp}_{\mathrm{LO}}(i)} \min\!\left( \left\lceil \frac{R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}}{T_k} \right\rceil,\; \left\lceil \frac{R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}}{T_k} \right\rceil \right) C_k^{\mathrm{lo}}
\]
where $\mathrm{hp}_{\mathrm{HI}}(i)$ and $\mathrm{hp}_{\mathrm{LO}}(i)$ are the
higher-priority HI-criticality and LO-criticality tasks, respectively. The
$\min(\cdot,\cdot)$ term bounds the interference from a higher-priority
LO-criticality task by the number of its jobs released before degradation
begins, since every LO-criticality task is abandoned on release once fully
degraded. The switch occurs at or before $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ within $\tau_i$'s
busy period, so $\tau_k$ releases at most
$\lceil R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}/T_k \rceil$ jobs; the first argument of the $\min$ is
the sound tightening that interference over a window of length
$R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ cannot exceed $\lceil R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}/T_k \rceil$ jobs
however early the switch occurs. A previous statement of this equation carried
a spurious $+1$ on the second argument, which the implementation never had. A task set is AMC-rtb schedulable if $R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}
\leq D_i$ for every $\tau_i \in \tau^{\mathrm{HI}}$ and $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}} \leq
D_i$ for every $\tau_i \in \tau$.

This is the two-level baseline this work compares against, and the degenerate
case ($k = 2$, every LO-criticality task abandoned at the single trigger) that
the general model in the next section must reduce to exactly.

\subsection{Severity-Indexed Response Time}
\label{sec:severity-response}

Generalise \Cref{sec:r-lo} by charging \emph{every} task at severity $\chi$
rather than only at $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$:
\[
R_i(\chi) = C_i(\chi) + \sum_{\tau_j \in \mathrm{hp}(i)} \left\lceil \frac{R_i(\chi)}{T_j} \right\rceil C_j(\chi)
\]
using the per-task budgets of \Cref{sec:severity-budgets}. If this recurrence
exceeds $D_i$ before converging, level $\chi$ is unreachable for task $i$ and
$R_i(\chi) = \infty$ -- a threshold that never fires, rather than the
partially-iterated value the recurrence had reached, which would depend on
where iteration happened to stop and break the monotonicity property below.

$R_i(\chi)$ answers a different question from $R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ in
\Cref{sec:amc-rtb}: it is the response time \emph{if every task, including
every LO-criticality one, were uniformly behaving at severity $\chi$ with none
yet abandoned}. That is deliberately more pessimistic than the true response
time once a drop policy is in effect, and pessimism in this direction is what
makes $R_i(\chi)$ safe to use as a run-time trigger: reaching it is evidence
that observed behaviour is \emph{at least} that severe, never a false alarm.
$R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$, by contrast, models the true operating response time under
the specific policy "abandon every LO-criticality task", and is a design-time
schedulability test, not something monitored at run time.

Three properties hold for every task set, and are what the multi-level
scheme's safety argument (\Cref{sec:admissibility}) rests on. They are checked
directly, not merely argued, in \texttt{tests/scheduling/test\_severity.py}.

\begin{description}
\item[(A)] $R_i(0) = R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ exactly -- a level at severity $0$
  triggers at exactly the classic single AMC trigger point.
\item[(B)] $\chi_a \leq \chi_b \implies R_i(\chi_a) \leq R_i(\chi_b)$ -- the
  severity-to-response-time map is monotone, so a ladder of increasing
  severities produces a ladder of non-decreasing triggers, given the
  $\infty$-saturation above.
\item[(C)] $R_i(\chi) \geq R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ for every $\chi$ -- no severity
  level ever triggers earlier than the classic AMC trigger.
\end{description}
\label{prop:c}

Property (C) is what an earlier fraction-based design ($\chi \cdot
R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ for $\chi < 1$, triggering \emph{below} the classic
threshold rather than at or above it) violated by construction: measured with
no HI-criticality behaviour present anywhere, such a threshold at $0.9 \cdot
R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ fired 14{,}118 times across twelve task sets, abandoning 356
jobs with no fault present. Charging every task's own budget, rather than
scaling the deadline, is what avoids this.

\subsection{Shed-Aware Response Time}
\label{sec:shed-aware}

$R_i(\chi)$ above charges every LO-criticality task in full, as though none
were ever shed. That is deliberately safe for a \emph{trigger}, but it is far
too pessimistic for an \emph{obligation}: the whole point of a drop set is that
the tasks in it stop releasing. Conversely, treating a shed task as vanishing
the instant its level is entered -- which is what the analysis in this document
originally did -- is optimistic, and unsound: abandonment is on release, so a
job already in flight runs to completion and still interferes.

Both are corners of one recurrence. Let $S$ be the set shed, and let $s_k$
bound the instant, measured from the start of $\tau_i$'s busy period, at which
$\tau_k \in S$ stopped releasing. Then $R_i(\chi, S, s)$ is the least fixed
point of
\[
w = C_i(\chi)
  + \sum_{\tau_j \in \mathrm{hp}(i) \setminus S} \left\lceil \frac{w}{T_j} \right\rceil C_j(\chi)
  + \sum_{\tau_k \in \mathrm{hp}(i) \cap S}
      \min\!\left( \left\lceil \frac{w}{T_k} \right\rceil,\;
                   \left\lceil \frac{s_k}{T_k} \right\rceil \right) C_k^{\mathrm{lo}} .
\]
The $\min(\cdot, \cdot)$ term is exactly the one AMC-rtb applies to the
two-level case, generalised to a per-task shed instant. Three specialisations
recover everything already defined, and are checked as an equality (not an
inequality) over $240$ HI-criticality tasks across $40$ task sets:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Specialisation & Yields & Meaning \\
\midrule
$R_i(0, \emptyset, \cdot)$ & $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ & \Cref{sec:r-lo} \\
$R_i(\chi, \emptyset, \cdot)$ & $R_i(\chi)$ & \Cref{sec:severity-response}, the trigger \\
$R_i(1, \tau^{\mathrm{LO}}, R_i^{\mathrm{lo}})$ & $R_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ & \Cref{sec:amc-rtb}, AMC-rtb \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Three monotonicity properties follow directly, and the third is what
\Cref{sec:ex-defer} illustrates:

\begin{description}
\item[(M1)] $R_i(\chi, S, s)$ is non-decreasing in $\chi$.
\item[(M2)] $R_i(\chi, S, s)$ is non-increasing in $S$ under inclusion, because
  $C_k^{\mathrm{lo}} = C_k(\chi)$ for every LO-criticality task.
\item[(M3)] $R_i(\chi, S, s)$ is non-decreasing in each $s_k$: \textbf{shedding
  earlier never hurts, and generally helps.}
\end{description}

\textbf{Where the shed instant comes from.} $s_k$ is not free. The rung that
sheds $\tau_k$ is entered no later than $R_i(\chi_{\ell_k + 1})$ within
$\tau_i$'s own busy period, because if $\tau_i$ is still incomplete at that
instant then $\tau_i$ fires the rung itself. The argument is available only to
a task that is permitted to fire that rung -- always true for a HI-criticality
task, and true for a retained LO-criticality task exactly when
$\ell_k + 1 \leq x_{\mathrm{LO}}$ (\Cref{sec:transition}). Where it is not
available, $s_k = \infty$ and $\tau_k$ is charged in full, as though never shed.

That caveat is not a technicality: $R_i(\chi) = \infty$ for $33\%$ of
HI-criticality tasks at $\chi = 1$, so a task shed at the \emph{deepest} rung
frequently has no finite bound at all. This is what drives the conclusion of
\Cref{sec:admissibility}.

\section{Multi-Level Degradation Model}
\label{sec:multilevel}

\subsection{Degradation Levels}

The system has $k \geq 2$ levels $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_{k-1}$, each assigned a
severity $0 = \chi_1 \leq \chi_2 \leq \dots \leq \chi_{k-1} \leq 1$ (level
$L_0$ has no severity; it is the absence of any triggered level). Pinning
$\chi_1 = 0$ is what makes $L_1$ trigger at exactly the classic AMC point
(property A) and is required by the safety argument in
\Cref{sec:admissibility}; $\chi_{k-1}$ need not be $1$, though setting it so
recovers the classic two-level scheme's full protection at the deepest level.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Degradation Level Structure}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
Level & Severity & Trigger Condition & LO Task Handling \\
\midrule
$L_0$ & -- & None & All tasks run at $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ \\
$L_1$ & $\chi_1 = 0$ & HI task reaches $R_i(0) = R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ & Drop set $S_1$ (Section~\ref{sec:admissibility}) \\
$L_2$ & $\chi_2$ & HI task reaches $R_i(\chi_2)$ & Drop set $S_2 \supseteq S_1$ \\
$\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ \\
$L_{k-1}$ & $\chi_{k-1}$ & HI task reaches $R_i(\chi_{k-1})$ & Drop set $S_{k-1} \supseteq \dots \supseteq S_1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each level's operating budget for every task is $C_i(\chi_x)$
(\Cref{sec:severity-budgets}): a task entering $L_x$ because severity $\chi_x$
was reached is granted exactly the execution allowance that severity
justifies, not immediately the full $C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ -- that is what makes
the degradation graded rather than binary. One consequence is worth stating
plainly: with $\chi_1 = 0$, $L_1$'s operating budget equals $L_0$'s. Entering
$L_1$ changes nothing about what any task is allowed to execute; it only marks
that an overrun has been confirmed somewhere; and it must still ask whether any
LO-criticality task needs to be shed to keep HI tasks safe at that (unchanged)
budget, which \Cref{sec:admissibility} answers per level. This is the same
principle Theorem 1 of the safety proof states for $L_0$ itself: protection
begins no later than the classic trigger, never earlier.

\subsection{Mode Transition Protocol}
\label{sec:transition}

Classically, only a HI-criticality task may pull the trigger:
\[
L_x \to L_{x+1} \quad \text{when } \exists\, \tau_i \in \tau^{\mathrm{HI}}:
  \text{active job reaches } R_i(\chi_{x+1}).
\]
\Cref{sec:ex-blindspot} shows what that misses. A HI-criticality task can
execute its full $C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}$ -- a genuine overrun -- and still complete
before $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$, because $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ budgets for interference
that a particular release need not receive. The overrun is then invisible to
every rung, while the LO-criticality task that absorbed it runs out of slack.

The generalisation is to let \emph{any} task that a rung would protect demand
that rung. Using the allocated levels of \Cref{sec:criticality-levels}:
\begin{align*}
L_x \to L_{x+1} &\quad \text{when } \exists\, \tau_i \text{ with } \ell_i \geq x+1
  \text{ and } (\kappa_i = \mathrm{HI} \text{ or } x+1 \leq x_{\mathrm{LO}}):
  \text{active job reaches } R_i(\chi_{x+1}) \\
L_x \to L_y &\quad y < x \text{ on exit (direct or cascade, per protocol design)}
\end{align*}
A task never fires a rung that would abandon it ($\ell_i \geq x+1$), so the
abandoned set is always a strict prefix of the criticality order.

\textbf{The threshold is calculated, not searched.} A LO-criticality task's
threshold for demanding rung $x$ is its own $R_i(\chi_x)$ -- the same quantity,
computed the same way, as for a HI-criticality task. Soundness is the same one
line: if every task had complied with $C(\chi_x)$, $\tau_i$ would have completed
by $R_i(\chi_x)$, so reaching it is evidence that behaviour is at least that
severe. Properties (A), (B) and (C) of \Cref{sec:severity-response} hold
verbatim for LO-criticality tasks, so no rung fires earlier than the classic
trigger and none fires with no fault present.

The mechanism is not vacuous even though $C_i(\chi) = C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ for
every LO-criticality task and every $\chi$: $R_i(\chi)$ still grows with $\chi$
through the HI-criticality tasks in $\mathrm{hp}(i)$. Measured over $199$ task
sets, $48\%$ of LO-criticality tasks have at least two distinct finite
thresholds (median spread $56\%$ of the smallest), and $83\%$ have a
higher-priority LO-criticality task available to shed.

\textbf{The parameter $x_{\mathrm{LO}}$.} It is the deepest rung a
LO-criticality task may fire, and it is a genuine dial rather than a
convenience. At $x_{\mathrm{LO}} = 0$ only HI-criticality tasks trigger and the
scheme provably abandons no more LO-criticality work than AMC-RH. Above $0$
that containment is lost -- \Cref{sec:ex-blindspot} is the counterexample --
and what is bought is protection for the higher-ranked LO-criticality tiers
against overruns two-level AMC cannot detect. The safety consequences are set
out in the accompanying safety proof; the trade is measured empirically rather
than settled by argument.

\textbf{Measured status of the mechanism.} The blind spot
\Cref{sec:ex-blindspot} describes is not an edge case. Across 60 task sets and
three seeds, at every overrun rate from $f_p = 10^{-3}$ to $f_p = 0.2$,
\textbf{$87$--$89\%$ of HI-criticality overruns fire no rung at all}: the job
executes beyond $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ and still completes before
$R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$. At $f_p = 0.2$ that is $129{,}874$ undetected overruns out
of $145{,}769$.

It does not follow that the mechanism pays, and measured over the same
population it does not: enabling $x_{\mathrm{LO}} = 1$ changes the service
ratio by less than $0.05$ percentage points and adds $20$ level entries in
$12{,}714$. The reason is specific and worth stating, because it is not the
reason one would guess.

\textbf{Why it does not engage.} A LO-criticality task fires a rung in order to
\emph{shed more work}. Under the shed-early policy that \Cref{sec:admissibility}
concludes is the only certifiable one, there is a single drop set: every rung
sheds the same tasks. Once $L_1$ has been entered there is nothing further any
deeper rung can shed, so escalating has no content. And escalation is almost
always already under way -- of $386$ terminations measured at $U = 0.7$ with
deadlines tightened to $30\%$ of the way from $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ to $T_i$,
$376$ occurred while the system was \emph{already degraded} and only $10$ at
$L_0$, the one state in which firing a rung would have changed anything.

Of those $386$, $298$ fell on tasks in the drop set -- in-flight jobs not
abandoned because abandonment is on release -- and $88$ on retained tasks.
The $88$ are the mechanism's entire addressable population, against a JNE loss
of $11.0\%$ of all jobs in the same configuration. Even captured perfectly it
would be a second-order effect.

\textbf{What would make it work.} The mechanism needs \emph{intermediate
tiers}: a task retained at $L_1$ and shed only at $L_3$ is one that escalation
can actually do something about. Intermediate tiers require progressive
shedding, which the single-phase carry-in bound of \Cref{sec:shed-aware} cannot
certify -- $130$ of $199$ task sets fail it, for the reason given in
\Cref{sec:admissibility}. The windowed two-phase analysis noted there as the
natural next step is therefore not merely a tightening: it is the prerequisite
for this mechanism having any content at all. Until it exists,
$x_{\mathrm{LO}}$ should be left at $0$, and the generalised trigger retained in
the model as the mechanism that certifiable intermediate tiers would activate.

The trigger point for the deepest level, $R_i(\chi_{k-1})$, is written
$R_{\mathrm{trigger}}$ where the level count is not otherwise relevant; with
$\chi_{k-1} = 1$ it recovers the classic AMC trigger for full degradation.

\section{Admissible Drop Sets and Safety}
\label{sec:admissibility}

Retaining LO-criticality work at an intermediate level necessarily means
absorbing interference that abandoning it would have avoided -- so the drop
set $S_x$ at each level is not a free design choice, it is an obligation
discharged by response-time analysis at design time.

\textbf{Definition (admissible drop set).} $S_x \subseteq \tau^{\mathrm{LO}}$
is \emph{admissible} at severity $\chi_x$ if, charging every task
$C_i(\chi_x)$, charging each shed task its carry-in per
\Cref{sec:shed-aware} rather than treating it as absent, and every retained
LO-criticality task at $C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every HI-criticality task $\tau_i$ has response time $R_i \leq D_i$, and
\item every retained LO-criticality task $\tau_j \notin S_x$ has response time
  $R_j \leq D_j$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Clause 1 is a constraint; clause 2 is an objective.} Clause 1 is what
certification requires and is always enforced. Clause 2 is not a safety
obligation at all under \Cref{sec:termination}: a retained LO-criticality job
that cannot finish is terminated at its deadline, so it never delivers a late
result -- it delivers none, which is the same outcome as being abandoned on
release and is counted identically by JNC. Requiring clause 2 therefore buys
\emph{certainty of service}, not safety.

It charges heavily for that certainty. A task the analysis cannot certify must
be shed outright, so every LO-criticality task is either guaranteed to complete
or never runs, with nothing in between -- and it is the state in between where
graded criticality actually lives. Over 199 non-trivial AMC-rtb-schedulable
task sets ($n=12$, $U=0.7$, $CF=2$), shedding at the shallowest rung with
carry-in charged:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Cost of enforcing clause 2}
\label{tab:clause2}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Clause 2 & LO tasks shed & LO tasks retained \\
\midrule
enforced          & 68.3\%  & 31.7\% \\
relaxed (default) & \textbf{47.6\%}  & \textbf{52.4\%} \\
\midrule
classic two-level & 100.0\% & 0.0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Relaxing it raises retention from $31.7\%$ to $52.4\%$ at no cost in
HI-criticality safety, because clause 1 is untouched and a terminated job
executes no more than it otherwise would.

An admissible set always exists
whenever the task set passes AMC-rtb (\Cref{sec:amc-rtb}): $S_x = \tau^{\mathrm{LO}}$
reduces clause 1 to the AMC-rtb HI-mode test and vacates clause 2, recovering
the classic two-level scheme exactly. So the question a drop policy answers is
never \emph{whether} a level is feasible, only how little must be shed --
which is what makes the drop set an optimisation rather than a guess, and is
addressed separately (drop-strategy research, out of scope for this document).

\textbf{Where the shed must happen.} Carry-in changes the design conclusion,
not merely the numbers. A task shed at rung $r$ stops releasing at
$R_i(\chi_r)$ for that rung's \emph{trigger} severity, and by
\Cref{sec:shed-aware} that bound is infinite for $33\%$ of HI-criticality tasks
at $\chi = 1$. Where there is no bound the shed task is charged in full, which
no amount of further shedding can repair -- so a rung that first sheds a task
deep in the ladder may be uncertifiable outright. Measured over $199$
non-trivial AMC-rtb-schedulable task sets ($n = 12$, $U = 0.7$, $CF = 2$) with
trigger severities $(0, 0.5, 1)$:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Shedding progressively against shedding at the shallowest rung}
\label{tab:shed-early}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Policy & Task sets uncertifiable & LO tasks shed \\
\midrule
Progressive (each task at its own rung) & 93 (47\%) & 71.5\% \\
Shed-early (one set, all at rung 1)     & 0 \phantom{9}(0\%) & 68.3\% \\
\midrule
Classic two-level (single trigger)      & 0 \phantom{9}(0\%) & 100\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

On the $106$ task sets where both policies are certifiable, shedding early
sheds strictly less on $12$, the same on $94$, and more on none. It is
therefore never worse, and it is feasible exactly when the task set passes
AMC-rtb -- shedding everything at $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ \emph{is}
\Cref{sec:amc-rtb}'s equation. So it never demands more than the classic test
and never rescues a set the classic test rejects.

The conclusion is that \textbf{grading belongs in the budgets, not in the drop
sets}. Progressive shedding buys nothing the analysis can certify and costs
certifiability on nearly half the population, which is the formal counterpart
of \Cref{sec:ex-defer}: a shed deferred to a deep rung arrives too late to help
the task it was meant to protect. The scheme still retains $31.7\%$ of the
LO-criticality tasks that two-level AMC abandons, with a proof that they meet
their deadlines.

For comparison, the figures obtained under the earlier assumption that
shedding takes effect instantaneously -- $0.0\%$ shed at $\chi \leq 0.25$,
$10.0\%$ at $0.50$, $33.7\%$ at $0.75$ and $56.3\%$ at $1.00$ -- are optimistic
by the whole carry-in term and are retained only as that comparison.

\textbf{Nesting.} $S_1 \subseteq S_2 \subseteq \dots \subseteq S_{k-1}$ is
required so that a level transition only ever adds to the drop set, keeping
the run-time decision incremental. This holds automatically for any drop
strategy that is a pure function of the task set and the remaining candidates
(such greedy strategies shed the same sequence of tasks regardless of
severity, so each level's set is a prefix of one sequence, and prefixes nest)
-- it is not an extra assumption on top of the definition above.

\textbf{Scope of this analysis.} The carry-in term of \Cref{sec:shed-aware}
discharges what was previously the largest gap here: the bound now matches the
simulator's abandonment-on-release policy, under which a job already released
when a level is entered runs to completion and still interferes. What remains
unmodelled is the interference profile \emph{before} a rung fires. The bound
charges every task at the rung's operating severity for the whole busy period,
including the interval in which the system was still at a shallower rung and a
lower severity would have sufficed. A windowed two-phase form -- charging
$C_j(\chi_{x-1})$ before the transition instant and $C_j(\chi_x)$ after, as
AMC-rtb does for the LO-criticality term -- would tighten this, and is the
natural next step. It is a source of pessimism, not of unsoundness: every
figure in \Cref{tab:shed-early} is an upper bound on what must be shed.

\section{Job Execution Model}

\subsection{Execution Time Distribution}

Each job's execution time is drawn at release:
\[
E_i \sim
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathrm{BCET}_i, C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}) & \text{if } \kappa_i = \mathrm{LO} \\
\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathrm{BCET}_i, C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}) & \text{if } \kappa_i = \mathrm{HI}, \text{normal behaviour} \\
\mathrm{Uniform}(C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}, C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}) & \text{if } \kappa_i = \mathrm{HI}, \text{HI behaviour}
\end{cases}
\]
HI-criticality behaviour occurs with probability $FP = 1/N$ per job.

\subsection{Execution Budget Enforcement}

A job is constrained by its own worst-case budget, not by the level the system
happens to be in:
\[
\text{Actual execution} = \min\!\left(E_i,\; C_i^{\mathrm{hi}}\right).
\]
$C_i(\chi_x)$ is an \emph{analysis} quantity -- what the response-time bound
charges at severity $\chi_x$ -- and never a run-time throttle. The distinction
is not cosmetic. Enforcing $C_i(\chi_x)$ at run time would cap a HI-criticality
job at $C_i(\chi_1) = C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ in the shallowest level, making an
overrun physically impossible; no threshold could then ever be reached and the
ladder would never leave $L_0$. Implementing that reading confirmed it exactly:
over a two-million-tick run with $f_p = 0.05$, $1{,}469$ jobs were truncated
and the system made zero level transitions.

For LO-criticality tasks $C_i^{\mathrm{hi}} = C_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$, so this is a
real cap on them and a defensive one on HI-criticality tasks, matching the
two-level model exactly.

\subsection{Deadline Termination}
\label{sec:termination}

A LO-criticality job that has not completed when its deadline arrives is
\emph{terminated}, not permitted to run on and complete late:
\[
\text{job } J_{i,q} \text{ of } \tau_i \in \tau^{\mathrm{LO}}
\text{ released at } r \text{ is discarded at } r + D_i \text{ if incomplete.}
\]

This is not a weakening of the timing guarantees; it is what makes them
meaningful. A late result from a control or monitoring task may be worse than
no result at all -- it is delivered into a system state it no longer describes.
Terminating is exactly the action two-level AMC already takes when it abandons
LO-criticality jobs on a mode change; only the moment differs, and the moment
is chosen so that the job never produces output it should not.

Three consequences follow, and \Cref{sec:objective} builds on all three.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A LO-criticality deadline miss becomes impossible by
  construction.} There is nothing left to certify about a retained
  LO-criticality task's deadline, which is why clause 2 of
  \Cref{sec:admissibility} is an objective rather than a constraint.
\item \textbf{HI-criticality safety is untouched, and if anything improves.} A
  terminated job executes no more than it otherwise would, so the interference
  a HI-criticality task suffers can only fall. Clause 1 is unchanged.
\item \textbf{Processor time can now be wasted.} A terminated job consumed
  execution that produced nothing. Abandonment on release never does this --
  it never abandons a job \emph{mid-execution} -- so WastedCPU was previously
  zero by construction and is now a live quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Objective: Jobs Not Completed}
\label{sec:objective}

The quantity to minimise is the work the system failed to deliver, however it
failed. Let $\mathrm{Exp}$ be the number of LO-criticality jobs a perfect
scheduler would have completed over a run of length $H$ -- a function of $H$
and the periods alone, and so independent of anything the scheme did:
\[
\mathrm{Exp} = \sum_{\tau_i \in \tau^{\mathrm{LO}}} \left| \{\, r = \phi_i + m T_i \;:\; m \in \N,\; r < H \,\} \right| .
\]
Let $\mathrm{Comp}$ be the jobs that actually completed within their deadlines.
Then
\[
\mathrm{JNC} = \mathrm{Exp} - \mathrm{Comp},
\qquad
\mathrm{ServiceRatio} = \frac{\mathrm{Comp}}{\mathrm{Exp}} .
\]

Defining JNC by \emph{difference} against a fixed reference, rather than by
summing the ways a job can fail, is deliberate: it cannot silently omit a
failure mode. A job delivers no result by being abandoned on release (JNE), or
by being terminated at its deadline having run -- and JNE alone, the metric the
two-level literature reports, counts only the first. It therefore
\emph{undercounts} once retained tasks are allowed to fail, which is precisely
the regime this model introduces.

\textbf{All metrics are reported normalised.} A raw count of abandoned jobs
scales with both the run length and the tasks' periods, so raw JNE is not
comparable between task sets or between experiments of different duration.
Every rate below is a percentage of $\mathrm{Exp}$; WastedCPU is a percentage
of $H$.

\section{Measured Performance}
\label{sec:results}

Service ratio against the two-level AMC-RA baseline on the same task sets,
arrival sequences and execution times. $k = 4$ with trigger severities
$(0, 0.5, 1)$, shed-early drop sets, $x_{\mathrm{LO}} = 0$, $f_p = 0.2$,
$200{,}000$ ticks $\times$ 3 seeds, 25 task sets per row. \emph{Tight} deadlines
are pulled down to $30\%$ of the way from $R_i^{\mathrm{lo}}$ to $T_i$, which
preserves normal-mode schedulability exactly while removing most of the slack.

Three things are worth reading off this table rather than asserting.

\textbf{The gain is real but modest}: $3.6$ to $5.0$ percentage points of
service ratio in the first three rows. That is the honest size of the effect
once shedding is charged its carry-in, and it is well below what the
instantaneous-shedding analysis implied.

\textbf{The hardest configuration is a loss.} At $U = 0.85$ with tight
deadlines the graded scheme is marginally \emph{worse} than two-level AMC
($0.6702$ against $0.6727$). Retaining LO-criticality work means absorbing
interference, and where almost a third of jobs are being shed anyway there is
no headroom left to pay for it. The scheme should not be recommended
unconditionally.

\textbf{Termination and waste are second-order.} Terminations never exceed
$0.24\%$ of jobs and wasted processor time never exceeds $0.32\%$ of the run.
The dominant loss is JNE throughout -- jobs abandoned on release because they
are in the drop set. Any further improvement therefore has to come from
shedding \emph{fewer} tasks, not from managing the ones that are retained: this
is the quantitative reason \Cref{sec:transition} concludes that the generalised
trigger cannot pay in its present form.
